# Cinematic Principles

## 1. Start with story

Describe a moment inside a sequence, not a static subject label. Establish a trace of the previous event and a direction for what comes next.

Weak: `A detective stands on a New York street.`

Stronger: `After the last patrol car leaves, a detective remains outside the shuttered grocery store, holding a coffee that has gone cold.`

Favor moments of waiting, aftermath, hesitation, interrupted routine, departure, or unobserved behavior. Use climactic action only when the brief depends on it.

## 2. Give the camera a physical witness position

Place the camera inside the same world as the subjects. Useful positions include:

- behind a half-open door;
- from the last row of a bus;
- across the street behind a shelter panel;
- from the back seat of a parked car;
- near floor level beside wet pavement;
- through scratched glass or a plausible reflection.

Obstruction is not automatically cinematic. Include it only when the chosen position would naturally produce it.

## 3. Make the environment carry evidence

Choose details that reveal place, age, recent events, or character state. Prefer a few causal details over indiscriminate clutter:

- a wet coat and water under a chair after rain;
- a cold drink and an overturned receipt after a conversation ended abruptly;
- worn seat edges, old advertisements, and maintenance marks in a long-used bus;
- raised shutters and delivery boxes at the start of a working day.

If the characters vanished, the location should still hint at what just happened.

## 4. Compose for observation, not promotion

Vary subject scale and position. Allow empty space, partial visibility, asymmetry, or environmental dominance when they express the scene. Faces need not always be frontal, complete, or perfectly sharp.

Avoid mechanically adding all imperfections at once. The composition should feel motivated, not randomized.

## 5. Motivate light

Name real sources before describing mood. A light description should answer:

- What is the primary source?
- What is the secondary or intermittent source?
- Where do shadows collect?
- Which surfaces catch highlights?
- What restrained warm/cool relationship follows from those sources?

Light serves the space. Do not use a blue rim, golden beam, volumetric fog, or flare merely as a style badge.

## Restraint Test

Remove all camera-brand, film-stock, resolution, and generic quality words. The remaining text must still describe a specific story, action, place, camera relationship, and source-based light pattern.
